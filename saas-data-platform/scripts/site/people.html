1 · ARR per employee

ARR per employee peaked at {{rpe_peak}}, then slipped to {{rpe_end}}.

ARR per employee by month

What it is

Company ARR divided by headcount at each month-end. The one number that joins the revenue and people domains.

Why it matters

Each hire supported more ARR until {{rpe_peak_month}}. In the six months after, the company added {{h2_hires}} people while ARR grew {{h2_arr}}, and efficiency fell back to where it was a year earlier.

What to do

Use ARR per employee as a guardrail on the next hiring plan: approve headcount against planned ARR so the ratio holds at $200k or better, and review it monthly next to the ARR bridge.

2 · Attrition

Customer Success loses {{cs_attrition}} of its people a year.

Voluntary attrition, last 12 months, by department

What it is

Exits in the last 12 months divided by average headcount, by department. Every Customer Success exit was voluntary.

Why it matters

Customer Success owns renewals, and churn tripled to {{churn_2025}} in the same year. The two may be linked, but this data can't show it yet. Pay isn't the obvious cause: the team is paid at its band midpoint.

What to do

Run stay interviews in Customer Success this quarter. Add the missing join, which CSM owns which account, so the next review can test whether accounts that lost their CSM churned more.

{{gap_l6}}

at L6, the level to watch next cycle

Women's compa-ratio versus men at the same level

What it is

Compa-ratio, salary divided by the band midpoint for the person's level and region, compared within each level. That makes it like for like.

Why it matters

The company average looks fair. At L4, women are paid {{l4_w_cr}} of their band midpoint and men {{l4_m_cr}}. A headline average would never show it.

What to do

Close the L4 gap in the next comp cycle, watch L6, and make this level-by-gender check a standing step in every comp review.
